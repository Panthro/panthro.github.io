Someone messaged me on LinkedIn while I was at N26. They wanted to apply but assumed the backend was mostly Java: verbose, legacy, not where they wanted to go. When I told them the reality was Kotlin microservices on Kubernetes, they changed their mind entirely.

That gap between outside perception and inside reality is why I wrote this. N26 had published a stack overview in 2018; by 2022 the picture had changed a lot. I had spent nearly three years inside it. This is the tour I wish I'd had on day one, written in September 2022 while I was still there. Numbers, tooling, and migration percentages evolve, because banks don't stand still. For what came later on financial crime and stream processing, see [Rebuilding Fraud Prevention from Scratch in Six Months](/articles/rebuilding-fraud-prevention-at-n26/).

## The shape of the system

N26 is a licensed EU neobank. That sentence matters more than any framework choice. Regulatory constraints, audit trails, and incident response are load-bearing walls in the architecture, not things you bolt on at the end.

## Containers everywhere

When I say everything lives in the cloud, I mean it literally, including the orchestration layers we outgrew.

We ran over **9,000 containers**. For a long time that meant Nomad plus a home-grown orchestration layer on SpotInst. It worked until it didn't. Pipelines built on the old setup had grown to **~1.5 hours** end to end: E2E tests, security gates, quality checks, monitoring hooks. Continuous deployment to production since 2017 only helps if the pipe stays fast enough to use.

Kubernetes became the obvious next step, because we needed predictable scheduling, a real ecosystem, and deployment times that didn't punish teams for shipping. By 2022 we were running it in all environments, with 80%+ of microservices migrated, typical deploys around 30 minutes and some pipelines under 15.

The migration wasn't painless. Kubernetes has sharp edges, especially when you're regulated and can't afford mystery failures at 2 a.m. But the direction was clear: faster, more stable deploys, and infrastructure that scaled with the business instead of fighting it.

## Kotlin, on purpose

A few years before this snapshot, N26 made an explicit bet on **Kotlin** for the JVM backend. It paid off.

When I joined, we were still in the mixed Java/Kotlin phase, with new services bootstrapped in Kotlin and older Java codebases in maintenance mode. By 2022, Kotlin was the default for new JVM work. Not because Kotlin solves every problem, since we still had Python, TypeScript, and Java where history or fit demanded it, but because the ergonomics matter at 230 services. Less ceremony, safer refactors, teams that actually wanted to open the repo.

The LinkedIn message wasn't wrong to care about language. It was wrong about *which* language dominated. Perception lagged reality by years, which is common at fast-moving companies and worth fixing publicly.

## CI/CD: separating build from release

We were mid-flight migrating from **Jenkins** to **GitHub Actions** for CI and **Argo CD** for CD. The split matters: build artifacts in one place, deploy intent in another, and a clearer audit trail for regulators who ask *what* shipped *when* and *who approved it*.

GitHub Actions handled test, scan, and container build in minutes, and native Kubernetes awareness made the migrated pipelines faster and less flaky than the Jenkins era. Argo CD handled declarative deploys across environments in under 30 minutes, with promotion paths aligned to our change-management rules. Monitoring-as-code alerts and SLO dashboards closed the loop, so when prod misbehaved the owning team's metrics fired first instead of a central on-call guessing.

It was early days for the migration, but the migrated pipelines were already winning on speed and stability. In a bank, "faster CI" isn't a developer convenience — it's how you patch fraud vectors before the weekend.

## Observability as infrastructure

Continuous deployment without observability is just faster breakage.

We invested heavily in **Datadog** for infra and app metrics, custom business metrics on top, and **ELK** for log access, with a move toward OpenSearch underway. The shift that actually changed behaviour was **Monitoring as Code**: alerts and dashboards living in the same repos as the services they watch.

Want to change an alert threshold? Open a PR on the service that owns the metric. Review it like code. Ship it like code. That sounds obvious until you've lived in a world where only three people can touch Grafana and everyone else pages them at midnight.

Three things followed from it. Engineers could reach production logs through the ELK stack without a ticket-based ritual, which matters when you're debugging a payment path at peak. Infra graphs told you the CPU spiked; business metrics told you card auths failed, and those carry different urgency and different runbooks. And because threshold changes went through PRs, the team that understood the failure mode owned the pager.

## Data: regulated, automated, valuable

Data at a bank is both asset and liability. N26's data org had to extract customer value from fraud signals, support automation, and product analytics without treating compliance as someone else's problem.

Our DBA function supported **100+ PostgreSQL** databases. We had migrated away from MySQL for most use cases, since PostgreSQL's versatility and operational maturity won. New database instances were provisioned through automation: **one PR, minutes to live**, auditable from creation.

That standardization let a small DBA team punch far above its headcount. Cloud-native Postgres, Infrastructure as Code, and ruthless elimination of snowflake setups. Machine learning had scaled past the lab too, with chatbot and related ML workloads running as part of daily operations.

## From HTTP coupling to events

~200 microservices talking to each other sounds elegant on a slide. In practice, synchronous HTTP everywhere builds a **distributed monolith**: change one service, coordinate five deploys, flip feature flags, hope nothing times out.

Our network moved **over 3 TB per day**, peaking around **10 TB**, with TCP latency usually under **5 ms**. HTTP is fast until every user request fans out into a tree of blocking calls and one slow dependency owns your p99.

We had async patterns early, with **AWS SQS** and **Kinesis** for workloads that fit. Kafka entered when we needed higher throughput, stronger ordering guarantees, replay for audit and recovery, and a path off the worst coupling points.

One concrete migration, moving a critical flow from synchronous HTTP to an async Kafka path, cut **p95 latency by ~50%**, **doubled throughput**, and **reduced load on a downstream system by ~66%**. At peak, several critical processes were doing **1,000+ messages per second** through Kafka. The gains compound past latency: consumers scale independently of producers, regulated workflows get replay and audit trails, and defining an event forces a domain modelling conversation that a REST call lets you skip.

We introduced Kafka gradually. Banks don't YOLO new infrastructure. Stabilize internally, learn failure modes, then expand. Plenty of systems were still on the roadmap to break apart, because event-driven architecture is a direction you travel rather than a box you tick.

## The honest part

If you've read this far, you're probably waiting for the catch. There is one.

We were **still** breaking down the original monolith, officially deprecated years earlier and unofficially longer. Macroservices from the early days still existed. Some integrations ran on third-party software with decades-old internals, cloud-hosted but old. Kubernetes migration was past 80%, not 100%. Kafka adoption was real but incomplete.

That's normal at scale. The mistake would be pretending otherwise in a recruiting blog post. What made the work interesting was watching a licensed bank modernize under regulatory load: card expiries hitting at once, subscription billing at scale, financial crime prevention (a topic big enough for its own article), and the daily grind of making deploys safe enough to run continuously.

Three things from that stack stuck with me. Perception lags reality, so publish the truth or lose candidates and partners to stale assumptions. Platform speed is product speed, because a 1.5-hour pipeline caps how fast you can respond to fraud, outages, and regulation. And monitoring-as-code moved alerts next to the people who understood the service, which changed incident culture more than any tool brand did.

## Questions

### What tech stack did N26 use in 2022?

Roughly 230 microservices, mostly Kotlin on the JVM, running on Kubernetes across all environments. Kafka, Kinesis, and SQS formed the event layer, 100+ PostgreSQL databases held the data, and delivery ran through GitHub Actions and Argo CD with Datadog and ELK for observability.

### Does N26 use Java or Kotlin?

Kotlin was the default for new JVM services by 2022, after an explicit company-wide bet made a few years earlier. Java remained in older codebases in maintenance mode, and Python and TypeScript were used where history or fit demanded it.

### How large was N26's infrastructure?

Over 9,000 containers under management, ~230 microservices, more than 3 TB of daily inter-service traffic peaking around 10 TB, and 100+ PostgreSQL databases provisioned by pull request.

### What did moving from HTTP to Kafka actually gain?

On one critical flow, migrating from synchronous HTTP to an async Kafka path cut p95 latency by ~50%, doubled throughput, and reduced load on a downstream system by ~66%. Several critical processes ran 1,000+ messages per second through Kafka at peak.
